# Biofuel Potential in Vietnam: Agricultural Feedstocks, Markets, Sustainability, and Deployment Priorities

**Prepared for:** Biofuels project, Vietnam  
**Author:** Manus AI  
**Assessment date:** 16 August 2026  
**Primary emphasis:** Agricultural residues and agricultural by-products, with comparison to crop-based liquid fuels and livestock-manure biogas

> **Executive conclusion.** Vietnam has a very large **gross agricultural-biomass resource**, but the portion that is simultaneously collectible, sustainable, affordable, and bankable is much smaller. The strongest near-term opportunities are **bagasse combined heat and power (CHP), rice-husk heat and power at mills and agro-industrial sites, selected rice-straw and maize-residue supply chains where collection and soil-retention safeguards are demonstrably workable, and manure/agro-industrial biogas**. Cassava-based ethanol has a stronger immediate market signal because nationwide E10 implementation began on 1 June 2026, but the implied feedstock requirement is large relative to domestic ethanol capacity and competes with starch, animal feed, food, and export markets. Vietnam should therefore expand biofuels through **residue-first, cluster-based, cascading-use strategies**, not through an indiscriminate expansion of dedicated energy crops.

## 1. Executive summary

Vietnam’s biofuel opportunity is entering a new policy phase. The Ministry of Industry and Trade states that E10 gasoline was introduced nationwide from 00:00 on 1 June 2026, while Circular 50/2025/TT-BCT provides the legal roadmap: E5 RON92 may continue through 31 December 2030, while other gasoline grades are to be blended at E10 nationwide from 1 June 2026.[1] [2] This creates a more predictable demand anchor for fuel ethanol than existed when the uploaded 2025 regional report described E10 as not yet adopted. The market signal is nevertheless not equivalent to domestic supply readiness. The U.S. Department of Agriculture’s Foreign Agricultural Service (USDA/FAS) cites an industry estimate of approximately 243 million US gallons of annual fuel-ethanol demand from the June 2026 rollout, against approximately 84 million gallons of domestic production and a potential gap of roughly 159–160 million gallons.[3]

The agricultural resource base is substantial. A 2025 Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada market overview, reproducing FAOSTAT statistics, reports 2023 production of approximately 43.498 million tonnes (Mt) of rice, 11.844 Mt of sugarcane, 10.377 Mt of fresh cassava, 4.437 Mt of maize, 2.132 Mt of coconuts in shell, and 1.957 Mt of green coffee.[4] The FAO’s latest agricultural-statistics release extends primary crop and livestock data through 2024, but the reproducible feedstock screening in this study uses the 2023 values because they are available in a consolidated Vietnam market table and can be paired transparently with the residue ratios and lower heating values in the uploaded Biomass Atlas.[5]

The most important result is not a single national potential number. It is the gap between potential definitions. The uploaded World Bank Biomass Atlas estimates approximately 59.89 Mt/year of theoretical crop-harvesting residues and 20.86 Mt/year of crop-processing residues. After existing uses, its crop-harvesting technical potential falls to approximately 15.22 Mt/year; after also considering farmers’ willingness to sell, it falls to approximately 7.95 Mt/year and 101,068 TJ/year.[6] The Atlas is a baseline rather than a 2026 forecast, but it demonstrates why gross residue totals should not be treated as fuel supply.

A screening recalculation using 2023 crop production and the Atlas ratios gives approximately 183,658 GWhth/year of gross energy for rice residues, 42,188 GWhth for maize residues, 17,606 GWhth for cassava residues, 11,515 GWhth for sugarcane residues, 3,793 GWhth for coconut residues, and 3,631 GWhth for coffee husk. Applying illustrative sustainable-availability fractions—not official forecasts—produces a combined range of approximately **40,501–84,936 GWhth/year** for these six crop families. At a simple 20–30% electricity-conversion screen, the equivalent is approximately **8,100–25,481 GWh/year** before project-level deductions for competing uses, moisture, storage, transport, conversion downtime, and grid or heat offtake. The largest uncertainty is not crop production; it is what can be removed and delivered without damaging soils or displacing existing economic uses.

The feedstock hierarchy differs by pathway. Rice straw is the largest gross category, but it is dispersed, seasonal, wet, and important for soil organic matter and circular uses. Rice husk is smaller in gross mass but more concentrated at mills and therefore more commercially tractable. Bagasse is highly concentrated and already integrated into sugar-mill heat systems, making it one of the most mature bioenergy pathways; however, existing use means additional electricity potential is lower than gross bagasse energy suggests. Cassava roots are a realistic ethanol feedstock, but cassava is not a residue: allocating more roots to fuel creates direct competition with starch, food, feed, and exports. Cassava peels, stalks, and wastewater are better candidates for integrated biogas, process heat, and biorefinery applications.

The recommended strategy is therefore to pursue four linked tracks. First, secure E10 through diversified domestic and imported ethanol supply while improving cassava productivity, plant utilization, and sustainability rather than assuming that all demand can be met from new cassava acreage. Second, scale bagasse CHP and industrial heat through sugar-mill modernization and better electricity/heat offtake. Third, develop rice-husk and selected rice-straw clusters in the Mekong River Delta and other rice-processing regions, using cooperatives, densification, storage, and soil-retention rules. Fourth, build manure and agro-industrial biogas/biomethane around dense livestock and processing clusters, where short collection radii and digestate use can make projects more resilient.

## 2. Scope, definitions, and method

This study treats “biofuel potential” as a chain rather than a resource inventory. It covers solid biomass for heat and electricity, liquid ethanol and biodiesel-type fuels, biogas and biomethane, and a brief assessment of advanced fuels and sustainable aviation fuel. Agricultural feedstocks are prioritized because they are central to Vietnam’s resource base and because residues can reduce land-use competition relative to dedicated energy crops.

The analysis uses four nested definitions. **Theoretical potential** is the energy contained in all estimated residues. **Technical potential** subtracts physical and technological limitations such as moisture, collection, conversion, and existing uses. **Sustainable potential** further retains material needed for soil carbon, nutrients, erosion control, biodiversity, animal feed, food security, and other environmental or social safeguards. **Commercially deployable potential** is the portion that can be contracted, transported, converted, financed, permitted, and sold at an acceptable margin.

| Potential level | Meaning in this study | Typical reason it is smaller than the previous level |
|---|---|---|
| Theoretical | All residues implied by crop output multiplied by residue-to-product ratios and energy content | Does not account for collection or competing uses |
| Technical | Material that can be physically collected, stored, and converted with available technology | Moisture, seasonality, dispersed farms, contamination, storage loss, and conversion efficiency |
| Sustainable | Material that can be removed while maintaining soil, nutrient, water, food, biodiversity, and social safeguards | Soil-retention needs, circular uses, animal feed, land-use and water constraints |
| Commercially deployable | Material that can be contracted and delivered to a bankable project and sold into a reliable market | Delivered cost, price volatility, offtake, permits, finance, grid, plant utilization, and policy risk |

The quantitative screening has three components. First, it uses 2023 crop production reported in the Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada Vietnam market overview, which draws on FAOSTAT.[4] Second, it applies the World Bank Biomass Atlas residue ratios and lower heating values reproduced in the uploaded AZEC/IEEJ Vietnam chapter.[6] [7] Third, it applies transparent low/high sustainable fractions to illustrate how quickly gross potential contracts once soil retention, existing markets, and collection constraints are acknowledged. These fractions are analyst-defined screening assumptions and must not be confused with a national resource assessment.

The calculation is:

> **Gross energy (TJ/year) = crop production (Mt/year) × residue ratio (t/t) × 1,000 × LHV (MJ/kg).**

The factor of 1,000 converts Mt multiplied by MJ/kg into TJ. Energy values are reported as GWhth using 1 GWh = 3.6 TJ. The accompanying CSV files contain all row-level inputs and outputs, and the Python script records the calculation logic.

## 3. Agricultural and energy-system context

Agriculture remains structurally important to Vietnam’s economy and rural employment. The uploaded AZEC/IEEJ Vietnam chapter reports that agriculture, forestry, and fishing accounted for 11.9% of GDP and 27.5% of employment in 2022.[7] This matters for biofuels because residues are generated by millions of farms, small processors, mills, traders, and livestock holdings rather than by a small number of centrally controlled resource owners. Feedstock policy is therefore also agricultural, rural-development, and industrial policy.

Vietnam’s crop mix offers several distinct residue systems. Rice generates large field residues and mill residues. Sugarcane generates bagasse and field trash, with bagasse already concentrated at mills. Cassava provides roots for ethanol and starch while generating peels, stalks, and processing wastewater. Maize creates substantial field residues and cobs, but maize is also a major feed input and Vietnam is a net importer of maize according to the 2025 market overview.[4] Coffee husk and pulp are concentrated around processing regions but can have soil-amendment, compost, feed, or local-fuel uses. Coconut residues are geographically concentrated in the Mekong Delta and other southern provinces but are dispersed across many small operations.

The country’s energy policy creates two different demand channels. The first is an immediate, mandatory liquid-fuel channel through E10 and continuing E5. The second is a planned biomass-power and waste-to-energy channel under PDP8 and its 2025 amendment, where actual deployment depends on project selection, tariffs, grid access, offtake, feedstock contracts, and financing. Circular 50 does not create an equivalent mandatory biodiesel blend for diesel engines; B5/B10 are encouraged, but diesel blending is excluded from the mandatory roadmap.[2]

| Demand channel | Current signal as of August 2026 | Implication for agricultural feedstocks |
|---|---|---|
| Fuel ethanol | Nationwide E10 implementation began 1 June 2026; E5 RON92 may continue to 2030 | Strongest immediate policy-led demand, but domestic production and feedstock supply are uncertain |
| Biomass heat and power | PDP8 and amended PDP8 provide capacity ambitions; current projects remain constrained by logistics and offtake | Best near-term prospects are clustered residues and captive industrial heat/CHP |
| Biogas/biomethane | No single national demand mandate comparable to E10; opportunity is driven by waste management, heat, power, transport, and gas substitution | Prioritize dense manure and agro-industrial organic-waste clusters |
| Biodiesel/renewable diesel | No mandatory national blend under Circular 50; B5/B10 are encouraged | Oilseed and waste-oil pathways are niche or longer-term unless a stronger offtake policy emerges |
| Sustainable aviation fuel and advanced fuels | Strategic long-term potential, but no national agricultural-feedstock demand anchor comparable to E10 | Begin with residues, wastes, certification, and demonstration-scale projects |

### 4.3 Feedstock-by-feedstock assessment

#### Rice straw

Rice straw is Vietnam’s largest agricultural residue opportunity by gross mass and energy. It is especially concentrated in the Mekong River Delta, where the uploaded AZEC/IEEJ chapter identifies the need for improved collection, transport, densification, and storage.[7] However, straw is not an unowned waste stream. It supports soil organic matter and nutrients, is used for mushroom production and organic fertilizer, and can be used for livestock or other circular-economy products.

The 2025 IRRI field report from Can Tho is important because it demonstrates a functioning alternative to open burning and a business model beyond fuel. A cooperative pilot produces approximately 50 tonnes per month of organic fertilizer and uses straw rolls for mushroom production; IRRI reports higher income than rice-only cultivation in the pilot and emphasizes financing for collection and processing machinery, cooperative organization, buyer connections, and technical support.[8] This suggests that rice-straw energy should be designed as part of a **cascade**: material with higher-value food, feed, or soil-amendment uses should not automatically be diverted to combustion.

The most credible near-term rice-straw model is therefore regional and seasonal: bale or densify only the surplus fraction after a soil-retention protocol, aggregate through cooperatives, use storage close to production zones, and contract a nearby heat, CHP, pellet, or biorefinery buyer. Long-distance transport of wet, low-density straw should be avoided unless densification and delivered-cost analysis demonstrate a margin.

#### Rice husk

Rice husk has lower gross mass than rice straw but higher commercial tractability because it is concentrated at rice mills. The AZEC/IEEJ chapter reports rice-husk prices in Can Tho of approximately VND 500–1,000/kg and briquette prices of approximately VND 1,500–2,000/kg.[7] The price evidence means husk is already a market commodity rather than a free feedstock. Projects must therefore compete with existing boiler, brick, briquette, bedding, and material uses.

Rice-husk energy is well suited to mill-scale boilers, CHP, gasification where technically justified, and nearby agro-industrial heat. Ash handling and silica-rich ash utilization must be included in the project design. A project should not assume that rice husk can be trucked economically over long distances simply because the annual national quantity is large.

#### Sugarcane bagasse and field trash

Bagasse is one of Vietnam’s strongest bioenergy feedstocks from a commercial-readiness perspective. It is generated at sugar mills, where it can supply process steam and electricity through CHP. The Biomass Atlas estimated roughly 600 MW of potential bagasse-based cogeneration at 40 surveyed sugar mills in the 2016–17 season.[6] The AZEC/IEEJ chapter also describes bagasse CHP as one of the more established biomass supply chains.[7]

The key limitation is not resource existence but allocation. Sugar mills already use bagasse for process heat, and surplus electricity depends on boiler efficiency, cane throughput, seasonal operation, and the attractiveness and reliability of grid offtake. The practical opportunity is to modernize boilers, improve steam integration, add high-pressure CHP, and sell surplus electricity or heat where contracts are bankable. Field trash and tops may add energy, but their collection is more difficult and soil-retention requirements are stronger than for bagasse.

#### Cassava roots, peels, stalks, and wastewater

Cassava is the most important agricultural feedstock for conventional fuel ethanol in Vietnam. The uploaded AZEC/IEEJ report states that cassava is viewed as more productive, lower-cost, and more efficient for ethanol than molasses or maize, while also warning that maize and molasses have competing uses.[7] The same report describes an unstable supply chain in which chips move through intermediaries and ports, and reports dry cassava chips at 60–64% of ethanol production cost.[7]

Cassava ethanol is technically credible but must be treated as a market-constrained root pathway, not as residue energy. A 2024 Vietnam-specific techno-economic and life-cycle study models a 94 million L/year cassava-chip ethanol plant and reports a modeled net-energy ratio of 1.70, production cost of US$0.40/L, and life-cycle emissions of 59.1 gCO2e/MJ, approximately 63% of gasoline emissions under the study boundary.[9] These are model outputs, not observed national averages, and cultivation, fertilizer, transport, plant utilization, and land-use change can materially change the result.

Cassava peels, stalks, and processing wastewater are more attractive for integrated circular systems than for separate nationwide collection. They can support anaerobic digestion, process heat, combined heat and power, and potentially advanced fuels after pretreatment. The best projects will co-locate ethanol or starch production with residue treatment, capture biogas, recover nutrients through digestate, and reduce wastewater liabilities.

The possibility of cultivating cassava on former mining land deserves targeted assessment. A 2024 feasibility study of a post-mining site in Vietnam frames this as a potential land-sparing niche and reports up to 49.3% CO2e savings relative to conventional RON92 gasoline under its scenario.[10] It also emphasizes that ordinary expansion on food-producing land can create food-versus-fuel and land-use risks. Post-mining cultivation should therefore be subject to soil and crop contamination testing, water-quality controls, food/feed exclusion where appropriate, and site-specific life-cycle accounting.

#### Maize residues

Maize residues produce a large gross energy figure in the screening, especially because the Atlas ratio for maize trash is high. The result should be treated cautiously. Maize fields are dispersed, collection is labor- and machinery-intensive, and much of the material supports soil cover or local livestock uses. More importantly, Vietnam’s maize imports and feed market make it inappropriate to treat maize grain as a preferred ethanol feedstock. Corn cobs and shells at aggregation or processing sites are more promising than field trash because they are denser and easier to collect.

A viable maize-residue project should begin with a documented cluster of shelling, drying, feed, or processing facilities. It should not begin with a national assumption that all maize trash can be baled and transported.

#### Coffee and coconut residues

Coffee husk and pulp can be valuable regional feedstocks in the Central Highlands, particularly for anaerobic digestion, compost, soil amendments, process heat, and pellets. Their suitability depends on moisture, seasonal accumulation, local environmental rules, and competing uses. The residue is concentrated at processors more effectively than field straw, but many processors are small and dispersed.

Coconut husk and shells are geographically concentrated in southern Vietnam. Shells have relatively high energy density and may be suitable for heat, charcoal/activated-carbon products, gasification, or pellets. Husk is more fibrous and costly to process. In both cases, a cascading-value assessment should compare fuel with material products, soil amendments, and local industrial heat.

#### Livestock manure and agro-industrial organics

Vietnam’s livestock base creates a major biogas opportunity. The 2025 agricultural market overview reports 25.5 million pigs, 6.33 million cattle, 3.09 million goats, 2.14 million buffalo, 457.2 million chickens, and 87.6 million ducks in 2023, in addition to substantial meat and milk production.[4] Manure is wet and spatially dispersed, so the most promising systems are farm clusters, centralized treatment around large livestock operations, and co-digestion with food-processing wastewater or crop residues.

A 2024 Vietnam study in *Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews* maps agricultural and livestock-waste biogas potential and models large cumulative emissions reductions under its scenarios, while reporting positive household-digester economics under particular assumptions.[11] Its results are scenario-based, so they should not be used as a national supply forecast. The IEA’s 2025 global assessment provides a useful logistics rule: manure’s high moisture typically limits economical centralized collection to roughly 10 km, while dense crop-residue areas may support collection radii up to 50 km; large digesters can be up to 40% cheaper than small systems.[12]

For Vietnam, biogas should be evaluated as a combined waste-management and energy project. The business case may come from avoided treatment costs, odor and pollution control, cooking fuel, industrial heat, electricity, biomethane, carbon value, and digestate fertilizer—not from gas sales alone.

## 5. Liquid biofuels and the E10 supply question

### 5.1 Ethanol demand after nationwide E10

Circular 50/2025/TT-BCT creates the strongest near-term national biofuel demand signal. The legal rendering states that from 1 June 2026 gasoline must be blended into E10 nationwide, with E5 RON92 permitted through the end of 2030.[2] MOIT’s announcement confirms the nationwide rollout and frames it as an energy-security and green-transition policy.[1] The USDA/FAS 2025 report cites annual gasoline demand above 10 million m³ and an industry estimate of 243 million US gallons/year of fuel-ethanol demand, compared with roughly 84 million gallons of domestic production.[3]

The following is a transparent screening conversion. It does not replace a formal fuel-demand model and uses the industry estimate rather than an official government forecast.

| Item | Industry estimate | Equivalent million litres | Dry cassava chips at 400 L ethanol/tonne |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Total annual ethanol demand | 243m US gal/year | 919.9m L/year | 2.300 Mt/year |
| Estimated domestic production | 84m US gal/year | 318.0m L/year | 0.795 Mt/year |
| Implied potential gap | 159m US gal/year | 601.9m L/year | 1.505 Mt/year |

The 400 L/tonne dry-chip conversion is an illustrative engineering assumption consistent with commonly reported dry-chip conversion ratios; plant mass balances, starch content, fermentation efficiency, denatured-ethanol specification, and coproduct credits must be used for investment decisions.[13] The implied 2.30 Mt/year of dry chips is therefore an order-of-magnitude requirement. It is large enough that ethanol policy, cassava prices, starch exports, animal feed, food security, and imports will interact. Vietnam cannot assume that an E10 mandate automatically creates a domestic cassava-ethanol surplus.

### 5.2 Domestic production, imports, and supply resilience

The uploaded AZEC/IEEJ chapter reports seven ethanol plants with total capacity of approximately 612 million L/year by 2019, but only two operational at that time; four had stopped and one had not reached operation, mainly because of low demand and financial difficulty.[7] E10 changes the demand environment, but it does not solve plant rehabilitation, financing, feedstock contracting, quality control, storage, blending infrastructure, or price risk.

The 2026 USDA/FAS update reports that the United States was the largest ethanol supplier to Vietnam in 2025, with US$4.3 million and 58% market share, followed by China at 26% and Japan at 6%; it also reports a reduction in Vietnam’s MFN ethanol tariff from 10% to 5% on 31 March 2025.[14] This indicates that imports are already part of the supply system and may remain important during domestic ramp-up. Import reliance is not necessarily a failure, but it exposes the E10 market to international prices, freight, exchange rates, trade policy, and supplier concentration.

A resilient ethanol strategy should maintain three buffers: a diversified import channel, a domestic plant-rehabilitation and efficiency program, and a sustainable feedstock-development program. Domestic supply should focus on productivity, logistics, plant utilization, and residue integration before encouraging additional root acreage on high-value food or forest land.

### 5.3 Biodiesel, renewable diesel, and sustainable aviation fuel

The current national policy signal is much weaker for biodiesel than for ethanol. Circular 50 excludes mandatory biodiesel blending, although it encourages B5/B10.[2] Vietnam has potential feedstocks in used cooking oil, animal fats, cassava and sugar-processing residues for advanced routes, and oil-bearing crops, but oilseed crops would need to be assessed against food and feed markets. Waste oils and fats are more consistent with the residue-first strategy than a large new land-based oilseed program.

Sustainable aviation fuel and advanced cellulosic fuels are longer-term opportunities. Vietnam’s rice, sugarcane, wood-processing, cassava, and municipal organic-waste streams could support demonstration pathways, but certification, hydrogen or process-energy requirements, feedstock traceability, airport offtake, and international fuel standards are major barriers. The immediate priority should be data, pilots, and certification-ready supply chains rather than a national volume target unsupported by demand or technology readiness.

## 6. Solid biomass, heat, and power potential

The original PDP8 and the uploaded AZEC/IEEJ report provide a historical 2030 biomass-power figure of 2,270 MW, comprising 1,088 MW of biomass plants and 1,182 MW of solid-waste plants, with a 2050 biomass/waste category of 6,015 MW.[7] The 2025 amended PDP8 changes the planning context. Secondary legal analysis and government-facing reporting indicate a 2030 biomass-power target of approximately 1,523–2,699 MW and waste-to-energy target of approximately 1,441–2,137 MW.[15] The range should be checked against the official Vietnamese decision and implementation plan when project-level decisions are made.

The key lesson is that a capacity target is not a feedstock contract. The AZEC/IEEJ chapter estimates that applying a 2020 biomass capacity factor to a 2,270 MW target would produce approximately 2,400 GWh in 2030, while bagasse alone might supply approximately 1,200 GWh to the national grid, leaving a gap that would require additional residues.[7] This illustrates why rice husk, rice straw, wood by-products, and other residues matter for diversification. It also illustrates why project developers should not count bagasse twice: once for existing mill heat and again for new grid electricity.

Vietnam’s 2025 Decree 58/2025/ND-CP provides a broader renewable-energy investment context, while the 2026 U.S. government market guide describes financial and regulatory incentives and a gradually more liberalized power market.[16] Biomass projects still require project-specific confirmation of tariffs, power-purchase arrangements, grid connection, land, environmental approval, fire safety, ash or digestate management, and feedstock contracts. The 2020 biomass tariffs cited in the uploaded AZEC/IEEJ report—VND 1,634/kWh for CHP and VND 1,968/kWh for non-CHP—should not be assumed to apply automatically to every new project without checking current legal applicability and contract terms.[7]

The best solid-biomass projects will generally be **captive or semi-captive**. A rice mill, sugar mill, coffee processor, starch plant, food factory, industrial park, or brick/ceramics facility can use heat directly, reducing dependence on a volatile wholesale-electricity market. Grid export can be an additional revenue stream, but it should not be the only one unless offtake and tariff risks are contractually controlled.

## 7. Regional deployment logic

A national residue total conceals the geography of supply and demand. Vietnam’s strongest near-term opportunities are cluster-based.

| Cluster type | Likely feedstocks | Preferred first projects | Principal safeguards |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mekong River Delta rice-processing cluster | Rice husk, selected straw, rice bran and organic by-products | Mill-scale boilers/CHP, densification, organic fertilizer and mushroom cascades, selected straw-to-energy pilots | Soil-retention rules, seasonal storage, cooperative contracting, ash and emissions management |
| Sugar-mill regions | Bagasse, cane trash, wastewater | High-pressure bagasse CHP, process heat, anaerobic digestion of wastewater | Preserve mill heat requirements; avoid double counting; seasonal operations |
| Cassava-processing regions | Roots, peels, stalks, wastewater | Ethanol/starch biorefineries with biogas, CHP, digestate, and wastewater treatment | Food/feed competition, land-use change, water, cyanogenic compounds, price volatility |
| Central Highlands coffee regions | Coffee husk, pulp, wastewater, pruning residues | Anaerobic digestion, compost, heat, pellets, small CHP | Protect soil-amendment uses; manage seasonal moisture and odor |
| Coconut-processing regions | Shells, husk, wastewater | Shell heat/char products, gasification or pellets, wastewater biogas | Compare energy with activated carbon/material value; small-processor aggregation |
| Dense livestock corridors | Pig and poultry manure, slaughterhouse and food wastes | Centralized or clustered digesters, CHP, biomethane pilots | Short collection radii, pathogen control, digestate nutrient management |

## 8. Sustainability and safeguards

The first safeguard is to distinguish **residue** from **waste**. A residue can have an existing function: straw returns nutrients to soil, husk fuels a boiler, bagasse supplies mill steam, cassava peels feed animals or enter starch processing, and coffee pulp can become compost. Diverting a residue to fuel can therefore create indirect emissions or economic losses even when the material is physically available.

The second safeguard is soil and nutrient retention. Vietnam’s agricultural policy context includes irrigation dependence, nutrient-surplus concerns, and climate-related water pressures. The OECD’s 2025 agricultural policy assessment describes the importance of maintaining rice production systems while improving nutrient, water, soil-health, and climate-smart management.[17] Projects that remove field residues should use field-level rules based on soil type, slope, organic matter, nutrient balance, yield, and alternative inputs; a flat national removal percentage is not sufficient.

The third safeguard is to prevent land-use change. Conventional cassava ethanol can have a positive energy and emissions balance under modeled conditions, but cultivation is a life-cycle hotspot and land expansion can compete with food or lead to indirect land-use change.[9] [10] Any new feedstock plantation should be screened for forest conversion, peatland or wetland impacts, food production displacement, water use, erosion, and chemical inputs. Former mining land may be a niche for non-food energy crops only where contamination, crop safety, hydrology, and long-term soil restoration have been demonstrated.

The fourth safeguard is emissions and worker/community safety. Biomass combustion requires particulate, nitrogen-oxide, carbon-monoxide, and ash management. Anaerobic digestion requires methane-leak monitoring, pressure and flare safety, pathogen control, and digestate management. The uploaded GIZ/MOIT environmental and social guidance should be used in project permitting and stakeholder consultation.[18]

The fifth safeguard is cascade use and benefit sharing. The IRRI rice-straw example shows that farmer income can increase through mushroom and fertilizer businesses rather than through fuel alone.[8] A good bioenergy project should disclose the price paid to farmers, the proportion retained for soil, the value of coproducts, local jobs, transport impacts, and how benefits are shared with cooperatives and processors.

## 9. Commercial and policy barriers

### Feedstock contracting and logistics

Vietnam’s residue supply is seasonal, fragmented, and often wet. A project must map suppliers within a realistic radius, measure moisture and contamination, quantify existing uses, and sign contracts before financial close. Aggregation machinery, covered storage, roads, weighing, quality testing, and inventory management are core infrastructure rather than ancillary costs.

### Price competition and opportunity cost

The relevant feedstock price is not zero. Rice husk already has reported local prices; dry cassava chips are a dominant ethanol cost; bagasse has internal value as mill fuel; maize residues can substitute for feed or soil cover; and coffee/coconut residues may have material or soil uses. Project models should compare delivered feedstock cost with the next-best use and include seasonal price spikes.

### Plant utilization and scale

The 2019 ethanol history shows that nominal capacity can greatly exceed operating capacity when demand and finance are weak.[7] Biomass plants face the same risk if a facility is designed for a national resource total rather than a contracted cluster. A smaller plant with high annual utilization and nearby heat offtake may outperform a large plant dependent on long-distance feedstock transport.

### Regulation and offtake

E10 has a clear policy demand anchor, but the feedstock and import system still needs stable quality, blending, storage, tax, tariff, and price mechanisms. Biomass electricity projects require current confirmation of tariff eligibility, power-purchase arrangements, grid access, and the amended PDP8 implementation pathway. Biogas and biomethane require standards for gas quality, injection or vehicle use, digestate, methane leakage, and environmental compliance.

### Data quality

The country needs a regularly updated biomass observatory or atlas using provincial production, mill locations, livestock density, residue prices, existing uses, transport networks, soil data, and project status. The uploaded Biomass Atlas cautions that processing-residue locations were sometimes proxied from crop production rather than actual mill locations, and that its surveys were not exhaustive.[6] These limitations become material when choosing project sites.

## 10. Recommended development program

### Priority 1: Secure the E10 transition without overbuilding dedicated feedstock supply

Vietnam should publish a transparent annual ethanol balance covering mandated demand, domestic plant capacity, actual production, imports, stocks, blending losses, and feedstock requirements. Domestic plants should be screened for rehabilitation, efficiency, water and wastewater performance, and integration with cassava-starch or biogas operations. The government and industry should avoid presenting all cassava production as available for fuel; the balance should explicitly show food, feed, starch, export, and farmer-income implications.

### Priority 2: Establish residue-first regional pilots

A first portfolio should include rice-husk mill energy, selected rice-straw cascades, bagasse CHP modernization, cassava-processing biogas, coffee-processing biogas/heat, coconut-shell valorization, and livestock-manure digesters. Each pilot should have a measured baseline, signed supplier contracts, a soil or nutrient protocol where field residues are removed, and a verified heat or electricity offtake.

### Priority 3: Build aggregation and storage infrastructure

Public and development finance should target shared balers, densifiers, storage warehouses, weighing and quality systems, cooperative-owned logistics, and digital contracting. The IRRI experience indicates that machinery finance and stronger cooperative-buyer links are decisive for rice straw.[8] Such infrastructure can serve both higher-value circular uses and energy markets.

### Priority 4: Use heat and CHP as the first commercial market

Project developers should prioritize direct industrial heat and CHP before merchant electricity or long-distance fuel markets. This is particularly important for sugar mills, rice mills, starch plants, coffee processors, food factories, and industrial parks. Heat offtake increases plant utilization and can reduce the burden on power tariffs.

### Priority 5: Develop biogas as waste treatment plus energy

Biogas projects should be designed around manure and organic wastes that have a treatment liability, with gas revenue as one component of the business case. Project design should follow short collection radii for manure, co-digestion where feedstock quality is complementary, methane-leak controls, and digestate nutrient plans. Biomethane pilots should target industrial heat, captive fleets, or other gaseous-fuel applications before assuming a national gas-grid market.

### Priority 6: Improve data, certification, and finance

Vietnam should update the Biomass Atlas at least annually or biennially, publish provincial feedstock and project data, and develop standard contracts for farmer cooperatives and processors. Sustainability certification should track origin, land-use change, soil-retention practices, moisture, emissions, and chain of custody. Carbon finance should be treated as supplementary rather than as the sole source of project viability.

## 11. Investment-screening checklist

A project should not proceed to detailed engineering until it can answer the following questions with measured evidence.

| Screening question | Minimum evidence expected |
|---|---|
| Is the feedstock actually available? | Twelve-month supply map, supplier list, existing-use survey, moisture and contamination measurements |
| Can it be delivered at a stable cost? | Radius analysis, roads, seasonality, storage, transport cost, price-sensitivity cases |
| Is removal sustainable? | Soil, nutrient, erosion, biodiversity, water, and farmer-consent protocol |
| Is the conversion pathway proven at this scale? | Demonstrated technology, mass and energy balances, local operating references |
| Is there a bankable offtake? | Heat, electricity, ethanol, gas, or product contract with price and volume provisions |
| Are permits and standards clear? | Environmental and social assessment, land, construction, grid, fuel-quality, emissions, and waste permits |
| Does the project survive downside cases? | Feedstock-price, utilization, exchange-rate, tariff, import-price, downtime, and carbon-price sensitivities |
| Are benefits shared locally? | Transparent farmer/processor pricing, jobs, cooperative role, grievance mechanism |

## 13. Limitations and interpretation cautions

The most important limitation is temporal. The crop-production baseline is 2023, while FAO’s latest release extends primary data through 2024. The policy baseline is current through August 2026, but some capacity and market statistics originate in 2024 or 2025. This is unavoidable when official crop, plant, and project-status data are published on different schedules; the study labels dates rather than combining them silently.

The second limitation is methodological. Residue ratios and lower heating values from the Biomass Atlas are useful for a transparent screening but do not prove that the same mass is collectible today. The model’s sustainable fractions are illustrative assumptions, not official estimates. Site studies should replace them with local measurements.

The third limitation is demand uncertainty. The 243 million US gallon ethanol estimate is attributed by USDA/FAS to an industry source, not an official demand forecast.[3] The 2026 USDA article’s reference to 2.9 billion gallons of IEA consumption is not interpreted here as ethanol demand because the underlying sentence is ambiguous.[14] Any investment model should build multiple demand cases and verify actual blended volumes after E10 implementation.

The fourth limitation is comparability. The Atlas potential, AZEC/IEEJ resource estimates, and this study’s recalculation use different crop years, categories, and deductions. They should be read as complementary evidence, not added together. In particular, bagasse, rice husk, and other residues may already be used for heat or products.

## 14. Conclusion

Vietnam’s agricultural biofuel potential is large enough to support a meaningful contribution to energy security, rural industry, waste management, and emissions reduction. The country has the raw materials: rice residues, sugarcane bagasse, cassava, maize residues, coffee and coconut by-products, and livestock manure. It also now has a much stronger transport-fuel policy signal through nationwide E10.

The resource opportunity is nevertheless smaller than gross biomass tables imply. Rice straw’s gross scale is offset by collection, seasonality, soil, and competing circular uses. Bagasse is commercially attractive but mostly tied to mill energy systems. Cassava ethanol is technically and economically plausible under modeled assumptions, but E10 could require roughly 2.3 Mt/year of dry chips under the cited industry-demand case, which would intensify competition with starch, food, feed, and exports. Biogas and biomethane offer a complementary route in dense livestock and agro-industrial clusters, especially where waste treatment and digestate value are included.

The strategic answer is **not one national feedstock**. It is a portfolio of regional systems: high-efficiency bagasse CHP; rice-husk mill energy; carefully governed rice-straw cascades; integrated cassava-starch/ethanol/biogas facilities; coffee and coconut processing clusters; and manure-based digesters. The decisive policy tools are reliable offtake, transparent feedstock data, cooperative logistics, sustainability rules, current tariff and permitting clarity, and finance that rewards measured performance rather than theoretical resource claims.

If Vietnam follows a residue-first and cluster-based approach, biofuels can become a practical part of the energy transition without turning agricultural land and food systems into an unpriced fuel subsidy. If it treats theoretical residues as freely available and assumes mandates alone will solve supply chains, the country risks repeating the underutilization, financial distress, and feedstock instability documented in the earlier ethanol experience.
